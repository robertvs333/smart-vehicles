Vehicle dynamics

Fundamental Vehicle Dynamics Equations

As a master’s student, you do not need to memorize every derived formula (like the full state-space matrix or the understeer gradient formula). Instead, memorize these fundamental physical and geometric relationships. Every complex model in this course can be derived from these building blocks.

1. General Equations of Motion (Vehicle Frame)

These are the core Newton-Euler equations governing the vehicle’s dynamic behavior in a moving reference frame. Because the frame itself is rotating (yawing), we get Coriolis acceleration terms (the \(v_y\omega_z\) and \(v_x\omega_z\) components).

  • Longitudinal Equation of Motion: \[m a_x = m(\dot{v}_x - v_y\omega_z) = \sum F_x\] Explanation: Balances the mass times longitudinal acceleration (including the centripetal effect of lateral velocity and yaw) with the sum of all external longitudinal forces.

  • Lateral Equation of Motion: \[m a_y = m(\dot{v}_y + v_x\omega_z) = \sum F_y\] Explanation: Balances the mass times lateral acceleration with the sum of all external lateral forces. This is your starting point for cornering analysis.

  • Yaw (Moment about Z-axis): \[I_{zz}\ddot{\psi} = I_{zz}\dot{\omega}_z = \sum M_z\] Explanation: Relates the vehicle’s yaw moment of inertia and yaw angular acceleration to the sum of moments generated by the tires around the center of gravity.

  • Vertical Equation of Motion (Static/Quasi-static): \[m a_z = F_{z1} + F_{z2} - mg = 0\] Explanation: Assuming no vertical acceleration, the sum of the vertical tire forces must support the vehicle’s weight.

  • Pitch (Moment about Y-axis): \[I_{yy}\dot{\omega}_y = F_{z2}b - F_{z1}a - (F_{x1} + F_{x2})h = 0\] Explanation: Assuming zero pitch acceleration, this moment balance is crucial for deriving the longitudinal load transfer (how weight shifts during braking or accelerating).

2. Single-Track (Bicycle) Model Force Balances

These equations sum the forces and moments specifically for the single-track model. They assume the steering angle \(\delta\) is small enough that \(\cos\delta \approx 1\) and \(\sin\delta \approx \delta\).

  • Sum of Longitudinal Forces: \[\sum F_{x,cg} = F_{x1} - F_{y1}\delta + F_{x2}\] Explanation: The total longitudinal force. Note that the front lateral tire force \(F_{y1}\) points slightly backward when steered by angle \(\delta\), creating a drag component.

  • Sum of Lateral Forces: \[\sum F_{y,cg} = F_{y1} + F_{y2}\] Explanation: The total lateral force is simply the sum of the lateral forces from the front and rear equivalent tires (ignoring the small longitudinal force component for small steering angles).

  • Sum of Yaw Moments: \[\sum M_{z,cg} = F_{y1}a - F_{y2}b\] Explanation: The turning moment around the CG, calculated by multiplying the lateral tire forces by their respective distances from the CG (\(a\) for front, \(b\) for rear).

3. Kinematics and Slip Angle Definitions

These geometric relationships couple the vehicle’s rigid body velocities with the localized velocities at the tires.

  • Steady-State Yaw Rate: \[\omega_z = \frac{v_x}{R}\] Explanation: For steady-state cornering on a constant radius \(R\), the yaw rate is directly proportional to the forward speed.

  • Vehicle Side Slip Angle: \[\beta = -\frac{v_y}{v_x}\] Explanation: The angle between the vehicle’s longitudinal axis and its actual velocity vector at the center of gravity (assuming small angles where \(\tan\beta \approx \beta\)).

  • Front Tire Side Slip Angle (Linearized): \[\alpha_1 = \delta - \frac{v_y + a\omega_z}{v_x}\] Explanation: The side slip angle of the front tire. It is the difference between the steering angle (\(\delta\)) and the actual path angle of the front wheel (which is affected by both lateral velocity \(v_y\) and the yawing motion \(a\omega_z\)).

  • Rear Tire Side Slip Angle (Linearized): \[\alpha_2 = -\frac{v_y - b\omega_z}{v_x}\] Explanation: The side slip angle of the rear tire. It is similar to the front, but there is no steering angle (\(\delta = 0\)), and the yaw effect opposes the lateral velocity (hence the \(- b\omega_z\)).

4. Linear Tire Model

To close the loop, you must link the kinematic slip angles to the dynamic forces.

  • Lateral Tire Force: \[F_{yi} = C_{F\alpha i}\alpha_i\] Explanation: The linear tire model assumes that for small slip angles, the lateral force generated by tire \(i\) is directly proportional to its slip angle \(\alpha_i\), scaled by the tire’s cornering stiffness \(C_{F\alpha i}\).
